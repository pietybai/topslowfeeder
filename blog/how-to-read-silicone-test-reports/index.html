How to Read a Silicone Test Report (LFGB, FDA, REACH)

Six fields on page one decide whether the report means anything for the product you are buying

Published: November 2026 · By Marvin Bai, Top Slow Feeder

Every few weeks a buyer sends us a screenshot of a certificate and asks one question: is this good enough for my market? Sometimes it is. More often the document proves something narrower than the cover suggests — a different grade of silicone, a different year, or a sample that is not the product being ordered.

This guide is the checklist we would want a buyer to run on our own documents. We will use our own LFGB, FDA and REACH report numbers as worked examples, including the places where those reports do not cover as much as a buyer might assume.

A test report is not a certificate

The word "certificate" gets used loosely in this industry. A certificate is usually issued by the factory or the brand — a one-page declaration saying the material meets a standard. A test report is issued by a laboratory, carries a report number, and shows what was tested, on which sample, on which dates, and what the measured result was.

When a supplier sends a certificate, ask for the underlying report. If there is no report behind it, there is no number to verify, and nothing to show a customs officer, an Amazon compliance team or a retail buyer.

Page one: the six fields that matter

Test reports are long — ours run from five to thirty-one pages — but almost everything you need to judge relevance is on the cover page.

  • The laboratory. Look for an accreditation mark and its number. CTI Huace, for example, prints CNAS L1910 and ilac-MRA on the cover; SGS prints its own scheme references. An unaccredited lab can still test, but the result carries less weight with European retailers.
  • The report number. It should appear on every page, and it must be quotable. If a supplier will not give you the number, you cannot verify the document or check it against later batches.
  • The sample name and model. This is the field buyers skip, and it is the most important one. "Solid silicone sheet, HT-91 series" is not the same claim as "slow feeder mat".
  • The material. Sheet, raw material, or finished article — the report says which, in writing.
  • The dates. Three of them: sample received, testing period, date of issue. A report issued in 2022 describes material from 2022. Standards, formulations and suppliers change.
  • The page count. "Page 1 of 7" tells you whether you are looking at a complete document or the first page of one. A single screenshot with no page count is not evidence of anything.

What each standard actually measures

LFGB (Germany, and the de facto EU reference). The report typically cites §30 and §31 of the German Food and Feed Code, together with BfR Recommendation XV for silicone. Two things are being tested: that the material does not release harmful substances in quantities that matter, and that it does not change the smell or taste of food. In our report A2260155444104001E, the sensory analysis and the volatile organic matter test are both recorded as pass.

FDA (United States). For repeated-use rubber articles the reference is 21 CFR 177.2600, which sets extraction limits in water and in a fat simulant. Our report A2260155444103001E states that the tested items comply with the limits for repeated-use rubber articles.

REACH SVHC (EU). This is a screening of the candidate list of substances of very high concern, against a 0.1% by weight threshold. It is a snapshot, not a permanent clearance: the candidate list grows, so the report has a shelf life. Our report A2260155444102001E records concentrations at or below 0.1% (w/w).

RoHS. Strictly speaking RoHS applies to electrical and electronic equipment, not to a silicone bowl. Buyers ask for it anyway, because it is the familiar restricted-substances list: lead, cadmium, mercury, hexavalent chromium, polybrominated biphenyls, polybrominated diphenyl ethers and four phthalates. Treat a RoHS report on silicone as a useful screening, not as legal compliance with a directive that does not apply to the product.

California Prop 65. A US state requirement rather than a product standard. Reports usually test a defined list of listed substances and state whether they were detected above the relevant safe-harbour level. Read which substances were tested before accepting the word "compliant".

The part most buyers miss: the sample is usually the material

This is where misunderstandings turn into disputes. Nearly every silicone report circulating in this trade — ours included — tests the silicone sheet the parts are moulded from, not the finished bowl or mat. Our reports cover grades HT-81, HT-91 and HT-99 sheet. That is useful and honest information: it tells you the compound in use has been screened. It does not tell you that a specific colour, a specific moulding, or a specific finished article has been tested.

If the report says "silicone sheet" and the purchase order says "printed mat in Pantone 7541", those are two different things. The gap is closed by asking which colours and components the supplier's report actually covers, and what happens for a colour that is not on that list. A supplier who answers that question precisely is worth more than one who forwards a PDF and says "all certified".

Five red flags

  • No report number, or a number that changes between documents.
  • Only a screenshot. One page, no page count, no lab letterhead. Ask for the full PDF.
  • Undated or expired. A five-year-old report on a material that has since been reformulated is decoration.
  • Sample name that does not match the product. The most common version: a material report presented as a product certification.
  • "Pass" with no measured values. A real report shows results or states the limit and the outcome per item, not a single word.

Verify the number before you trust the document

A report number is only useful if it can be checked, and this takes about two minutes.

  • Same number on every page. Open the PDF and page through it. A document where page one carries one number and the results pages carry another has been assembled from more than one source.
  • Use the QR code on the cover. Most Chinese laboratories now print a QR code that resolves to their own verification page, where the report number and the tested sample are shown. Scan it, and compare what the page says with what the PDF says.
  • Search the laboratory's own site. CTI, SGS, TÜV and Intertek all offer a report lookup by number. If the number returns nothing, ask the supplier to confirm it in writing.
  • Check the sample field against your order. The verification page will show the sample name the laboratory recorded. That is the field that tells you whether you are looking at material or a finished article.

None of this requires an account or a fee, and it is the fastest way to separate a real report from a recycled PDF with a new logo on it.

Five questions to send your supplier

  • Can you send the full report PDF, including the conclusion page, with the report number visible?
  • Which material grades and which colours does this report cover?
  • Is the sample a raw material or a finished article?
  • What is the issue date, and does its coverage still match the current formulation?
  • If my colour or size is not covered, what do we do — extend the report, or re-test?
